      Methods and caveats

      Pores. In “Tap examples” each pixel is converted to CIELAB colour and assigned to whichever examples, pore or grain, it is closest to; the balance slider shifts that boundary. Pores are grouped by 8‑connectivity. Size and shape come from each object’s pixels: equivalent circular diameter (ECD) from area, axes from the moment ellipse, Feret diameters and solidity from the convex hull, and circularity as 4πA/P² with a corner‑corrected perimeter.

      Grains. Grain‑forming pixels (not pore, not matrix or cement) are first split into many small regions by a watershed on the smoothed distance map, steered by an edge map that combines colour edges with thin dark contact lines. Neighbouring regions are then merged step by step, but only where the contact between them is wide relative to the smaller region, carries no visible edge, and joins similar colours. Leftover fragments join the neighbour they share the longest contact with. Small pores enclosed inside a grain are counted as part of it when most of the pore space is open; for packed loose grains, where enclosed gaps are real pore space, they are left alone. Tested on synthetic sections with known grains, 87–96% of grains are recovered with an overlap of 70% or more when contacts or colour differences are visible, and about 70% when touching grains look identical.

      Matching the Python notebook. “Use the same method as the Python notebook” runs K‑means on RGB with k‑means++ seeding, gives every pixel its group’s role, then erodes and dilates with a disk (radius in original‑image pixels) and labels pieces with 4‑connectivity, keeping every piece. Checked against scikit‑learn, scipy and scikit‑image on six test images at full resolution, grain counts agree to within about 1% for five of them (for example 630 against 629 pieces) and within 10% for the sixth, where K‑means settles slightly differently. Results only match when both work on the same pixels: browsers can’t hold a whole rock‑slab image in memory, so large images are analysed at reduced size unless you analyse an area at full resolution.

      Colour groups and erode & dilate. “Colour groups” clusters every pixel into K groups with K‑means (in perceptual CIELAB colour, or raw RGB), and groups given the same role are merged into one phase. “Erode & dilate” is a morphological opening with a disk of radius r, computed exactly from distance maps, followed by 8‑connected labelling; it cuts bridges thinner than about 2r, removes grains narrower than 2r, and trims corners unless trimmed edges are given back. The region table lists area, axes, centroid and bounding box for every object.

      Image types. The first guess clusters the image’s colours and picks the blue epoxy phase if there is one, otherwise the darkest phase. Dark‑epoxy and crossed‑polarised images work, but grains at extinction look like pores, and loose‑grain photos measure the shadowed gaps between grains rather than true porosity. “Even out uneven lighting” removes broad brightness gradients before classification.

      Interpretation. Section sizes are apparent 2D sizes and are smaller on average than true or sieve sizes. Folk and Ward statistics are number‑weighted from section ECDs; sorting and skewness don’t need a scale, the mean does. Areal porosity approximates volumetric porosity but misses microporosity below image resolution.
